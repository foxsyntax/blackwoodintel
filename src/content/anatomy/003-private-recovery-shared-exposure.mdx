<AnatomySection num="Opening" title="The Claim Is Not the Cash" pathogens={["Verification gap", "Rights-recovery divergence"]}>

A claim can be legally transferable and still be almost impossible to turn into cash. Ownership does not settle whether an obligation exists, whether it has been paid, or whether anyone can enforce it.

Three moves in Radiant World's 2026 dispute show how wide that gap can get.

- **Jefferies-linked fund (LAM).** Negotiated an early-April forbearance with Radiant and, according to an affidavit attributed to Christopher Bushell and reported by Bloomberg, accepted limits on contacting Radiant's buyers. That is the fund's own account; the signed agreement has not been seen. [S2]
- **Deutsche Bank, Singapore branch.** Used a contract clause to take about $11.25m from Radiant's accounts in August (set-off). [S1]
- **Mizuho.** Demanded repurchase on 12 August, issued a statutory demand on 17 August, and applied on 26 August for judicial management. Interim managers were appointed on 24 September. [S1, S4]

All three had money at risk. They did not have the same options. We can see what each creditor did but not why, so the useful question is about options:

What set the routes available to each creditor apart, and which of them were still worth anything once the underlying claims were disputed?

<AnatomyPull>A creditor's position depends on how valid its claim is, but also on which rights it can still use, when it can use them, and how other creditors' moves change them.</AnatomyPull>

</AnatomySection>

<AnatomySection num="I" title="The Asset That Could Not Be Taken at Face Value" pathogens={["Verification gap"]}>

An invoice records what a buyer owes a seller. A financier lends against it or buys it. Repayment then depends on whether the buyer really owes an unpaid, enforceable debt, whether ownership passed properly to the financier, and whether the financier has anywhere else to turn if either fails.

Radiant's own lender table lists approximately $870m utilised across six financiers against about $1.42bn of limits. It was attached to a 16 September affidavit of Pinkesh Nahar; the spreadsheet is undated and covers Radiant World Corporation only. [S9] The figures cannot be interpreted without distinguishing reported utilisation from contractual demands and recoverable exposure: Deutsche was quoted describing its $102.59m as maximum exposure, and Intesa's listed $238.26m differs from its August demand of about $126.15m. [S1, S9]

Two cases in the court record show face value and recoverable value coming apart.

- **Deutsche and Glencore.** One Glencore-linked receivable (face $17.1138m) had a transaction reference Glencore recognised. Glencore's evidence was that it had already been paid and that the version Deutsche held was materially different: dated 12 March, with different terms and an assignment. [S1, 44–52]
- **Mizuho and Glencore.** Real contracts with matching reference numbers do not prove that the specific receivables financed were valid. Glencore pointed to material differences in the invoices and underlying contracts, including seller identity, transaction details, payment terms and assignment provisions. [S1, 27, 32] A Mizuho court filing reported by Bloomberg says that an email Radiant forwarded after 5 August, purportedly a reply from a Glencore iron ore operations manager, was one he said he had not sent, and that Glencore's analysis was that the correspondence had been "generated fraudulently". Reuters reported Glencore saying Radiant sent fabricated emails purportedly from its personnel to several financial institutions. Radiant denies wrongdoing. [S10, S11]

Six Vitol-linked receivables at Deutsche are a third case: Vitol said it did not recognise them, which raises the question whether the debt existed as documented. [S1]

An invoice may describe a real transaction without representing a debt that remains collectible. The defects differ, and each raises its own question about the buyer's obligation and may change which recovery routes stay open, subject to the financier's contractual protections.

</AnatomySection>

<AnatomySection num="II" title="The First Trade-Off: Preserve the Borrower or Verify the Claim?" pathogens={["Borrower preservation trade-off", "Verification gap"]}>

|  | Jefferies-linked fund (LAM) | Intesa |
|---|---|---|
| **Information** | Queried overdue Glencore invoices on 4–6 February. [S3] | Suspicion began in April, after Radiant proposed a repurchase because a buyer was said to have payment problems. [S1, 61–63] |
| **Move** | Early-April forbearance; reportedly limited from contacting buyers (single source, fund's own account). Renewed correspondence with Glencore in June and July. [S2, S3] | Checked buyers directly, who did not recognise the invoices. Police report 7 May. Statutory demand of about $126.15m in August. [S1, 61–63] |
| **Unseen** | Forbearance terms, protections received, what the fund concluded about validity | Whether escalation improved its recovery |

Put the two files side by side and a conflict appears. The same accommodation that keeps a repayment route alive may restrict the creditor's ability to verify what it is being repaid for. Independent verification may strengthen knowledge of a claim while making continued accommodation harder. Early warning does not necessarily translate into stronger recovery rights.

Whether either approach improved the creditor's recovery position remains unknown. The full contractual terms, contemporaneous assessments and eventual recoveries are unavailable, and the two creditors faced different decisions: the fund was already chasing a buyer over overdue invoices, while Intesa was testing a seller's explanation.

So the question worth asking is about the trade:

<AnatomyPull>What did the creditor gain in exchange for limiting its access to independent confirmation?</AnatomyPull>

The restriction is a reported term, and the fund's own account says Radiant's lawyers demanded it. The record does not show whether the fund received protections such as cash visibility, repayment milestones, guarantees or retained enforcement rights.

</AnatomySection>

<AnatomySection num="III" title="Verification Is Not Transferable Confidence" pathogens={["Confidence non-transferability", "Verification gap"]}>

Mizuho is the clearest case. It signed its facility on 11 June and paid $95,514,771 for five Glencore-linked receivables on 18 June. [S1] Its paperwork required invoices, contract and performance evidence, and a notice of assignment sent to Glencore, with the acknowledgement coming back to Radiant by email — meaning the buyer's acknowledgement travelled back through the seller. [S1, 14] The email Radiant later forwarded is in section I. The judgment does not say what other checks Mizuho made, or whether it knew of Intesa's report.

Four distinct questions arise when confidence in a financial claim is formed, relied upon, or transferred:

- **Who is vouching for it?** The seller: Radiant presents an invoice or acknowledgement.
- **Who has confirmed it?** The buyer, ideally through a channel the seller does not control.
- **Who has accepted it?** The financier, which decides to buy or lend.
- **Who would trust it next?** A later buyer would need to form its own confidence in the claim. This stage is an extension of the analysis; there is no evidence that Radiant's receivables were resold.

<AnatomyAxiom label="Core finding">The transfer of a claim does not guarantee the transfer of confidence in that claim.</AnatomyAxiom>

Confidence is also no substitute for enforceability: a claim can be trusted and still be impossible to enforce, and a legal right can still be hard to sell or collect. Confidence in a claim is one condition among several, alongside enforceability, how easily the claim can be sold, timing and creditor coordination.

That matters for the next holder. A subsequent holder cannot necessarily reproduce the original financier's confidence unless the underlying evidence remains independently accessible and verifiable. No later buyer's behaviour is in evidence, so this is an inference from the Mizuho facts.

</AnatomySection>

<AnatomySection num="IV" title="Rights That Could Still Be Exercised" pathogens={["Rights-recovery divergence"]}>

A right can exist, be exercised, and still fail to produce a recoverable dollar.

| Creditor | Remedy exercised | What limits it |
|---|---|---|
| **Deutsche** | Repurchase notices of 5 and 7 August; account debits of about $11.25m; statutory demand. [S1, 48, 53] | Whether the set-off holds up; ranking against other creditors; challenge risk if Radiant becomes insolvent |
| **Mizuho** | Repurchase demand of 12 August ($97.28m); statutory demand 17 August; judicial management application 26 August. [S1, S4] | Radiant's ability to pay; turning assets into cash; the move to court-supervised proceedings |
| **LAM** | London freezing order (application 27 August, order reported by 2 September); Singapore freezing injunction after the 9 September hearing. [S6–S8] | Scope of the orders not examined; a freeze protects assets but does not pay |

The comparison here is about what each creditor could do. The record does not establish any creditor's final net recovery.

Deutsche had both contractual demands and direct access to Radiant's accounts, and it used both. Mizuho held an express repurchase right and exercised it, and the record shows no equivalent debits; whether the demand produced cash depended on Radiant paying. Account debits, freezing injunctions and judicial management affect creditor positions in different ways: through control of cash, restrictions on asset dealings, or court-supervised administration.

Between having a right and getting paid sit three steps: legal entitlement (the contract or the law gives a right), practical executability (it can be used against the right party and asset at the right time), and economic realisation (it yields value that survives the dispute, the costs and other creditors' claims).

<AnatomyPull>The creditor best positioned to enforce a contractual right is not necessarily best positioned to realise its economic value.</AnatomyPull>

For anyone writing similar deals, three questions carry over. Can the claim be confirmed without relying on the seller? If the buyer disputes the debt, which of your rights still produce real money? Which of your rights could other creditors' moves change?

</AnatomySection>

<AnatomySection num="V" title="When Private Protection Can Threaten Shared Recovery" pathogens={["Collective action feedback", "Rights-recovery divergence"]}>

On Radiant's own evidence, the court recorded that several financiers and trading partners froze accounts, made demands and stopped dealing within a short period, disrupting trading and potentially weakening cash flow. It reasoned that independent managers had a better chance of preserving the value of contracts still running than an immediate liquidation would. [S1, 72–78]

Alongside this, and only as a prima facie view, the court recorded a pattern of fraudulent conduct, said Intesa's experience "comports with and contributes to the pattern of fraud", noted that Radiant's management accounts were unreliable, and did not treat the roughly $2bn claim against Glencore as a certain recoverable asset. Radiant denies wrongdoing. [S1, 69, 73, 74]

Put together, these facts are consistent with a feedback loop:

*Disputed receivables → creditor-specific protective actions → potential disruption of trading and cash flow → possible deterioration of seller-dependent recovery → a stronger case for considering independent asset administration.*

<AnatomyPull>In some conditions, enforcing a claim can weaken the very business from which repayment is expected.</AnatomyPull>

That inference has two limits. The disruption may also have followed from the allegations and the conduct alleged, and the court does not divide its causes between creditors' actions and everything else. The same assessments also raise doubt about how much real value Radiant's receivables and operations held. Nothing here shows that creditors caused a specific collective loss.

This is why judicial management matters here. Mizuho, a private creditor, applied for it, and it passes control from Radiant's management to court-appointed professionals, moving part of the response from private enforcement toward collective administration. The record does not show whether Mizuho chose it over other routes.

</AnatomySection>

<AnatomySection num="Conclusion" title="The Right That Survives" verdict={true} verdictSub={"Creditor position is not exposure size.\nIt is which rights still produce value\nonce confidence in the claim has fallen."}>

In Radiant, claims became disputed while the means of turning them into recovery were spread across creditors with different documented information, contractual protections and access to assets. Whether information passed between them is still unknown.

Once confidence in the underlying obligations fell, the headline size of a creditor's exposure no longer described its position. What mattered was which claims could still be proved, which remedies could still be used, and whether using them protected or eroded the value repayment depended on.

<AnatomyAxiom label="What would change this view">This is a reading of the record so far. If differences in verification, contractual recourse and asset control turn out to have made no material difference to whether recovery routes stayed open or kept their value, the central distinction fails. If the disruption to Radiant's trading is shown to have arisen independently of what creditors did, the feedback loop in section V fails with it.</AnatomyAxiom>

<AnatomyAxiom label="Core axiom">The rights that preserve an individual claim do not necessarily preserve the value on which other creditors' recoveries depend.</AnatomyAxiom>

</AnatomySection>
